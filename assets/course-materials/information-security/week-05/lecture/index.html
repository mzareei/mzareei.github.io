Asymmetric Encryption & Public-Key Algorithms

How do two strangers agree on a shared secret… in plain sight of everyone?

The problem it solves

The key-distribution problem

  • Symmetric encryption (AES, etc.) is fast — but both parties must share the same secret key beforehand.
  • How do you send that key to someone you've never met, over a network that might be wiretapped?
  • With n users wanting pairwise conversations, you need n(n−1)/2 keys. For 1000 users: nearly half a million.
Provocation

You want to send a secret to someone across the world whom you've never met. You can't meet. Any message can be read by an eavesdropper. Is this even possible? In 1976, the answer went from "no" to "yes."

The big idea

Two keys instead of one

In public-key (asymmetric) cryptography, each person has a pair of keys: a public key they share with everyone, and a private key they never reveal. What one locks, only the other unlocks.

01

Modular arithmetic

The mathematical machinery that makes all public-key cryptography possible.

Clock arithmetic

What does "modulo" mean?

  • a mod M is the remainder when a is divided by M. Numbers "wrap around" when they reach M.
  • You already use this: a 12-hour clock is arithmetic mod 12. 10 o'clock + 5 hours = 3 o'clock (because 15 mod 12 = 3).
  • Public-key algorithms are built on three modular operations:

Addition

(a + b) mod M

Multiplication

(a × b) mod M

Exponentiation

(ab) mod M

Addition & additive inverse

Modular addition: encrypt and undo

  • Additive inverse of k: the number that, added to k (mod M), gives 0.
  • Example with M = 10: the inverse of k = 2 is k⁻¹ = 8, because 2 + 8 = 10 ≡ 0 (mod 10).
  • It is reversible by adding the inverse — perfect for decryption.
// encrypt: plaintext p=3, key k=2
c = p + k = 3 + 2 = 5   (mod 10)
// decrypt: add the inverse k⁻¹=8
c + k-1 = 5 + 8 = 13 = 3 = p   (mod 10)
◆ Activity · Additive inverse

What is the additive inverse of 8 mod 20?

Find the number that added to 8 gives 0 (mod 20).

Answer

12, because 8 + 12 = 20 ≡ 0 (mod 20). In general, the additive inverse of k is simply M − k.

Multiplication & multiplicative inverse

Modular multiplication: the inverse is trickier

  • Multiplicative inverse of x: the number y such that x × y ≡ 1 (mod M).
  • With M = 10: 3 and 7 are inverses of each other, because 3 × 7 = 21 ≡ 1 (mod 10).
  • Only some numbers have an inverse. With M = 10, the numbers 2, 4, 5, 6, and 8 do not.
1 ✓3 ✓7 ✓9 ✓ 2 ✗4 ✗5 ✗6 ✗8 ✗
Key rule

A number x has a multiplicative inverse mod n if and only if x is relatively prime to n (they share no factor other than 1).

How to find the inverse

Euclid's (extended) algorithm

  • Given x and n, the extended Euclidean algorithm finds y such that x × y ≡ 1 (mod n) — the multiplicative inverse.
  • It first computes the greatest common divisor (gcd). If gcd(x, n) = 1, the inverse exists; otherwise it doesn't.
  • It is efficient: it works even for huge numbers hundreds of digits long — which is why it's practical in real cryptography.
gcd(x, n) = 1   ⟹   the inverse exists
◆ Activity · Multiplicative inverse

What is the multiplicative inverse of 3 mod 17?

Find y such that 3 × y ≡ 1 (mod 17).

Answer

6, because 3 × 6 = 18 ≡ 1 (mod 17). Since gcd(3, 17) = 1, the inverse exists (17 is prime, so every number from 1 to 16 has an inverse).

Euler's totient function

Totient function φ(n)

φ(n) counts how many integers smaller than n are relatively prime to n (sharing no factor other than 1).

n is prime

φ(n) = n − 1 (every smaller number is coprime).

n = p × q, both prime

φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1) — the heart of RSA!

p, q coprime

φ(p × q) = φ(p) × φ(q) (multiplicative).

◆ Activity · Totient

Compute φ(21) and φ(30)

Hint: factor each number into primes first.

Answer

φ(21): 21 = 3 × 7 (both prime) ⟹ (3−1)(7−1) = 2 × 6 = 12.
φ(30): 30 = 2 × 3 × 5 ⟹ (2−1)(3−1)(5−1) = 1 × 2 × 4 = 8.

Modular exponentiation

The totient trick for large exponents

xy mod n = x(y mod φ(n)) mod n
  • Reducing the exponent modulo φ(n) makes gigantic powers tractable — the key to computing RSA quickly.
  • Special case: if y mod φ(n) = 1, then xy mod n = x mod n.
Why it matters

Computers never compute the full xy (it would have millions of digits). They use "fast exponentiation" (square-and-multiply), reducing mod n at every step.

Worked example 1

Compute c = 7²¹ mod 10

Use the totient technique, step by step.

110 = 2 × 5, both prime ⟹ φ(10) = (2−1)(5−1) = 4.
2Reduce the exponent: 21 mod 4 = 1.
3So c = 71 mod 10 = 7.
Worked example 2

Compute c = 7²⁷ mod 30

130 = 3 × 10, and 3 and 10 are coprime, so φ(30) = φ(3) × φ(10).
2φ(3) = 2; φ(10) = 4 (since 10 = 2 × 5) ⟹ φ(30) = 2 × 4 = 8.
3Reduce the exponent: 27 mod 8 = 3.
4So c = 73 mod 30 = 343 mod 30 = 13.
02

Diffie-Hellman key exchange

The first published public-key algorithm (1976).

A historic breakthrough

Diffie and Hellman, 1976

  • The first published public-key algorithm, together with the very exposition of the public-key concept.
  • A practical method to agree on a secret key securely over an open channel — that key then encrypts messages with a fast symmetric cipher.
  • Used in countless commercial products; its security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm.
Analogy

Picture mixing paints. You and I publicly agree on a base color (yellow). Each of us secretly adds our own color and mixes. We publicly swap the mixtures. Each of us adds our secret color again — and we both reach the same final mix. An eavesdropper saw the mixtures, but "un-mixing" paint is prohibitively hard.

The protocol

How the exchange works

Public numbers: prime q (≥ 300 digits) · primitive root α (often small)

User A (Alice)

elige secreto XA < q
calcula YA = αXA mod q
→ envía YA a B
secreto = YBXA mod q

User B (Bob)

chooses secret XB < q
computes YB = αXB mod q
← sends YB to A
secret = YAXB mod q
User C (eavesdropper): sees q, α, YA, YB — but to find the secret would have to compute XA = dlogα,q(YA): the discrete logarithm, computationally infeasible.

Both sides reach the same value because (αXA)XB = (αXB)XA = αXAXB mod q.

Worked example

DH with q = 23, α = 5

Alice picks a = 6; Bob picks b = 15. Follow the steps.

1Alice sends A = 56 mod 23 = 15625 mod 23 = 8.
2Bob sends B = 515 mod 23 = 19.
3Alice computes Ba = 196 mod 23 = 2.
4Bob computes Ab = 815 mod 23 = 2.
✓Both now share the secret key 2 — which never traveled across the network.
Why is it secure?

Diffie-Hellman security

  • The shared key (the secret) is never transmitted — each side computes it locally.
  • For Y = αX mod q, given Y, α and q, finding X is the discrete logarithm problem.
  • Conjecture: when q is a huge prime, computing X is practically impossible with classical computing.
Y = αX mod q    // easy →   // ← reversing is very hard
What DH does NOT do

Limitations and the man-in-the-middle attack

🐌

Expensive

Exponentiation is costly: possible DoS attacks and extra communication overhead.

🔁

Key exchange only

It does not encrypt anything by itself — it only establishes a shared secret key.

🎭

No authentication

It doesn't verify who you're talking to, so you can't sign anything — and it's open to a man-in-the-middle.

MITM attack

Mallory sits between Alice and Bob: she runs DH separately with each. Alice thinks she shares a key with Bob, but shares it with Mallory; same for Bob. Mallory decrypts, reads, and re-encrypts everything. The fix: authenticate the public values (signatures, certificates).

DH in the real world

Diffie-Hellman today: ECDHE and forward secrecy

  • In practice we use ephemeral DH over elliptic curves: ECDHE, with a fresh key per session.
  • This provides forward secrecy: if the server's long-term key is stolen tomorrow, yesterday's recorded sessions stay protected.
  • TLS 1.3 (2018) uses (EC)DHE for every session — it's what protects every HTTPS connection you open.
Connection

Today's tiny q=23 example and your bank's TLS connection use exactly the same math — just with numbers hundreds of digits long.

03

RSA (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman)

Encryption and signatures with a key pair — 1977.

What RSA is

RSA: general characteristics

  • Widely used and one of the first (1977); supports both public-key encryption and digital signatures.
  • Theoretical basis / security assumption: factoring a very large integer is hard.
  • Plaintext is treated as an integer that must be smaller than the modulus n; the ciphertext block is the same size as the key.
Key sizes

Variable key length: 512, 1024 (legacy / broken) and 2048, 4096 (in use). More on safe sizes shortly.

The four operations

How does RSA work?

Public key KU = ⟨e, n⟩ · private key KR = ⟨d, n⟩.

Encryption

c = me mod n   (m < n)

Decryption

m = cd mod n

Signature

s = md mod n   (m < n)

Verification

m = se mod n

Note the symmetry: encryption uses the public key e; signing uses the private key d. A signature is "encryption in reverse."

Key generation, step by step

Why does RSA work? Generating the keys

1Pick two primes: p = 17, q = 11.
2Compute n = p × q = 17 × 11 = 187.
3Compute φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) = 16 × 10 = 160.
4Choose e coprime with φ(n)=160 and smaller than it: e = 7.
5Find d with d × e ≡ 1 (mod 160): d = 23, because 23 × 7 = 161 = (1 × 160) + 1.

Result: public key (7, 187), private key (23, 187).

◆ Activity · Encrypt with my public key

My public key is (e, n) = (7, 187)

Take the last 2 digits of your student ID as the message m and encrypt them with my public key!

c = m7 mod 187
Method

Use fast exponentiation, reducing mod 187 at each step. Example with m = 2: 27 = 128, and 128 < 187, so c = 128. Only I, holding d = 23, can recover m via m = c23 mod 187.

◆ Activity · Verify my signature

You receive m = 100 with my signature s = 145

Using my public key (7, 187), did it really come from me?

verify: m =? se mod n = 1457 mod 187
Answer

Compute 1457 mod 187 = 100. Since it matches the received message, the signature is valid: only the holder of the private key d could produce an s that "decrypts" to 100 under the public key.

◆ Activity · Full RSA

Given p = 3 and q = 11, with e = 7

Compute n, φ(n), d, and write the public and private keys.

Answer

1) n = 3 × 11 = 33. 2) φ(n) = (3−1)(11−1) = 2 × 10 = 20. 3) d × 7 ≡ 1 (mod 20) ⟹ d = 3 (because 3 × 7 = 21 = 20 + 1). 4) Public key (e, n) = (7, 33). 5) Private key (d, n) = (3, 33).

◆ Activity · Encrypt & decrypt

Public key (7, 33), private (3, 33), message m = 2

What is the encryption of m? What formula decrypts m?

Answer

Encryption: c = 27 mod 33 = 128 mod 33 = 29. Decryption: m = cd mod n = 293 mod 33 = 24389 mod 33 = 2 ✓ — we recover the original message.

◆ Activity · Challenge

p=17, q=11, e=59, c=96 — recover the message

n = 187, φ(n) = 160. Find the private key d, then decrypt c = 96 to recover the plaintext m.

Method hint

Find d by solving 59 × d ≡ 1 (mod 160) with the extended Euclidean algorithm (d = 19, since 59 × 19 = 1121 = 7 × 160 + 1). Then m = cd mod n = 9619 mod 187, using fast exponentiation. The structure is the point: knowing p and q ⟹ φ(n) ⟹ d breaks the system.

The security core

Why is RSA secure?

  • Factoring a large integer is extremely hard for a classical computer.
  • If you could factor n into p and q, you'd break RSA from the public key ⟨e, n⟩:
1Know the factors p, q such that n = p × q.
2Compute φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1).
3Find d with e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) — you now have the private key!
Debate

RSA's entire security rests on an unproven assumption: that factoring is hard. No one has proven it is. Would you bet the world economy on a mathematical conjecture? (We do, every day.)

2025 update

RSA key sizes: what's safe today

Key sizeStatus
512-bitBroken. Factorable in hours on consumer hardware.

Takeaway

Old course lists include 512 and 1024 as valid options. Today they are not: use ≥ 2048-bit, or better still ECC with much smaller keys.

"Schoolbook" RSA is dangerous

Why you never use raw RSA in production

Deterministic

With the same key, a given plaintext always maps to the same ciphertext — an attacker can recognize repeated messages or test guesses.

Worse: messages 0, 1, and −1 encrypt to 0, 1, and −1 regardless of the key.

Malleable

Transforming the ciphertext transforms the plaintext predictably. For c = me mod n, the attacker changes c to se × c…

…and the receiver gets s × m instead of m, without ever decrypting the message.

The fix: padding

PKCS and OAEP: adding randomness

  • The Public-Key Cryptography Standards (PKCS) define how to pad the message before encrypting.
  • OAEP (Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding) prepends padding seeded from random bytes to the message m.
  • The result: encrypting the same message twice yields different ciphertexts — goodbye determinism and malleability.

04

Beyond RSA

Digital signatures, elliptic curves, and the post-quantum future.

Signatures, not encryption

Digital Signature Standard (DSS / DSA)

  • DSS uses SHA together with the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA).
  • Proposed in 1991, revised in 1993 over security concerns, and again in 1996.
  • Cannot be used for encryption or key exchange: it is designed only for the digital-signature function.
Today

Classic DSA is deprecated. Modern signatures use ECDSA or EdDSA (Ed25519): faster, smaller, and more secure.

Same security, tiny keys

Elliptic-Curve Cryptography (ECC)

  • Based on the mathematical structure of an elliptic curve: y² = x³ + ax + b.
  • Offers the same security as RSA with far fewer bits — a 256-bit ECC key ≈ 3072-bit RSA.
  • Appears in standards such as IEEE P1363; the original material warned that confidence in ECC was "not yet as high" as RSA.
Update

That caveat is now outdated: ECC is today's default. Curve25519 / Ed25519 secure SSH, Signal, TLS and more — small, fast, and heavily vetted keys.

The threat on the horizon

Post-quantum cryptography

  • A large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would break RSA, Diffie-Hellman and ECC — all three at once.
  • In August 2024, NIST finalized the first post-quantum standards:

ML-KEM

Kyber · FIPS 203 — key encapsulation (replaces DH-style exchange).

ML-DSA

Dilithium · FIPS 204 — lattice-based digital signatures.

SLH-DSA

SPHINCS+ · FIPS 205 — hash-based signatures.

"Harvest now, decrypt later"

An adversary can record your encrypted traffic today and decrypt it years from now, once a quantum computer exists. That's why post-quantum migration starts now, not when the machine arrives.

05

Pulling it together

The essentials — and one important correction.

Important correction

"RSA and Diffie-Hellman are the only public-key algorithms"

This claim, common in older materials, is false.
— correction to the original slides
  • ECC (elliptic-curve cryptography) is public-key — and dominates today.
  • ElGamal (encryption and signatures) is public-key too.
  • Lattice-based post-quantum schemes (ML-KEM, ML-DSA) are public-key and represent the future.
Lesson

RSA and DH are historically important and still in use, but far from the only ones. Always question absolute claims in security.

Takeaways

Summary in one slide

  • Modular arithmetic (inverses, Euclid's algorithm, totient φ(n), exponentiation) is the foundation of all public-key cryptography.
  • RSA encrypts and signs; its security assumes factoring a large integer into two primes is hard. Raw RSA is deterministic and malleable — always use OAEP padding and keys ≥ 2048-bit.
  • Diffie-Hellman is for key exchange; its security assumes the discrete logarithm is hard. Without authentication it's open to a man-in-the-middle — so in practice we use authenticated ECDHE.
  • ECC gives the same security with tiny keys; post-quantum cryptography (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) prepares for the day quantum computers break RSA, DH, and ECC.
◆ Closing debate

Trusting civilization to a conjecture?

All online banking, messaging, and commerce rest on the assumption that factoring and the discrete logarithm are hard — something never proven. Is this wise? What happens the day someone disproves it?

Discussion points

• Practical security is always computational, not absolute: "hard with known technology." • Crypto-agility (being able to swap algorithms) matters as much as the algorithm itself. • Quantum computers make this concrete: that's why NIST already standardized post-quantum alternatives. • Defense in depth: never rely on a single mathematical assumption.
