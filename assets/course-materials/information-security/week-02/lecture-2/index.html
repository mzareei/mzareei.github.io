Access control

Once we know who you are — what are you allowed to do?

Definition

What is access control?

“The process of granting or denying specific requests to: (1) obtain and use information and related information-processing services; and (2) enter specific physical facilities.”
— NISTIR 7298
Note

It's not only digital: a lock, a turnstile, and a security guard are access control too.

A big idea

At its core, almost all security is access control

Computer security: “measures that implement and assure security services in a computer system, particularly those that assure the access-control service.”
— RFC 4949
Discuss

Do you agree that "all security is access control"? Where do encryption or availability fit into that claim?

01

Who can access what

The model: subjects, objects, and the matrix.

Two parts

Access control = policy + enforcement

📜

Part I — Policy

Decide who should have access to which resources.

🛂

Part II — Enforcement

Ensure that only the accesses defined by the policy are granted.

Essential

Complete mediation is key: every access must be checked, no shortcuts. One unguarded door defeats the whole policy.

The vocabulary

Subjects, objects, and access rights

👤

Subject

An entity capable of accessing objects. Classes: owner, group, world.

📄

Object

A resource whose access is controlled (file, database, device, room).

🔑

Access right

How a subject may use an object: read, write, execute, delete, create, search.

Read it

Read it as a sentence: subject has right on object — "Ana may read payroll.xlsx".

The central abstraction

The access control matrix (ACM)

  • The rows are subjects/users/groups.
  • The columns are the resources to protect.
  • Cell ACM[U, O] says what rights user U has on object O.
Analogy

Picture a giant spreadsheet: one row per person, one column per door, each cell saying "may enter / may not".

Example

A concrete matrix: who enters which room

User \ ResourceRoom 1Room 2Room 3Room 4
Daniel✔✘✘✘
Jessica✘✔✔✘
Jose✔✔✘✘
Max✔✘✔✔
Roberto✘✔✘✔
Discuss

5 users × 4 rooms is already 20 cells. 10,000 accounts × 50,000 files = 500 million cells, mostly empty. That's why we never store the matrix directly — that sparseness is exactly why ACLs, capabilities, and RBAC exist.

One matrix, two views

The same matrix, sliced by column or by row

Now the cells hold rights (r = read, w = write, x = execute). Slice a column and you get an ACL; slice a row and you get a capability list.

Subject \ Objectnomina.xlsxapp.binreporte.pdf
Ana (HR)r wr xr
Beto (dev)—r w xr w
Cris (auditor)r—r

Blue column → ACL of nomina.xlsx

Ana : r w Cris : r (Beto: no access)

Violet row → Beto's capabilities

app.bin : r w x reporte.pdf : r w (nomina.xlsx: none)
The ACL lives with the object; the capability list travels with the subject. Same information, two ways to slice it.
02

How to store the matrix

By column (ACL) or by row (capabilities).

Two ways to slice the matrix

Access control list (ACL) vs. capability list (C-list)

📋

ACL — per object (column)

Each resource carries the list of who may access it and how. It lives with the object (e.g., in the file's metadata).

🎟️

C-list — per subject (row)

Each user carries a catalogue of capabilities: unforgeable handles to the resources they may use.

Analogy

ACL = the guest list at the door (the object knows who's allowed). C-list = the key or ticket in your pocket (you carry the proof).

◆ Quick question

ACE or capability?

Ana goes to a movie theater and buys a ticket for her favorite film. She's let in because she holds the ticket. The ticket is more like a…

1 — Access control entry (ACE)

2 — Capability

Answer

2 — Capability. The ticket is carried by the subject and proves their right (C-list). An ACE would sit at the door, tied to the object (the room), listing who may enter.

Trade-offs

Each approach wins at something

CriterionACL (per object)C-list (per subject)
Efficiency ("what can this user use?")✘✔
Accountability ("who can touch this object?")✔✘
Revocation (removing an access)✔✘
Why

An ACL instantly answers "who can access this file?" and revoking is deleting one line. A C-list instantly answers "what can this user use?", but collecting every key already handed out is hard.

Implementation

Where do they live?

The ACL

In the trusted part of the system, alongside the object's metadata. Made of access control entries (ACEs). A file's metadata already holds much of this.

The capability

An unforgeable reference to a resource. The user's catalogue defines what they can access; it can even be stored with the objects themselves.

Think

"Unforgeable" is the key word: if you could fabricate a capability, you'd have access to anything. How do real systems achieve it? (cryptography, kernel-protected tables…)

03

Who decides the policy?

DAC · MAC · RBAC · ABAC

Four models

Four ways to govern access

DAC — Discretionary

By the requestor's identity and rules; the owner decides.

MAC — Mandatory

Compares security labels with clearances; the organization decides.

RBAC — Role-based

By the user's roles and the rules for each role.

ABAC — Attribute-based

By attributes of the user, the resource, and the environment.

Quick analogy

DAC is the key to your own front door; MAC is a government clearance you can't override; RBAC is the badge tied to your job title; ABAC is airport security checking your ticket, your ID, and your gate, all at once.

Explore it yourself

The four models, side by side

Tap DAC, MAC, RBAC, or ABAC: see how it decides access, a concrete example, and a typical use case.

🗝️

How it decides

The resource's owner grants or revokes access to specific users, at their discretion.

📎

Example

Ana owns budget.xlsx and shares it with Beto as “view only” from the Google Drive sharing menu.

💻

Typical use case

Personal cloud files; default file permissions on most desktop operating systems.

🏷️

How it decides

The system compares the object's classification with the subject's clearance; no individual can override it.

📎

Example

A file marked Secret can only be opened by someone with a Secret clearance or higher — no exceptions.

🏛️

Typical use case

Military and government classified systems; SELinux in enforcing mode.

🪪

How it decides

Permissions attach to roles, not people; a user gains access by being assigned to (or activating) a role.

📎

Example

The "Nurse" role can view patient vitals; assigning Wendy to the "Nurse" role grants that access instantly.

🏥

Typical use case

Hospitals, banks, enterprise IT — wherever job functions are stable and well defined.

🧩

How it decides

A policy engine evaluates subject, resource, and environment attributes together, on every request.

📎

Example

“Allow if subject.department == resource.department AND time is 09:00–18:00 AND device.managed == true.”

☁️

Typical use case

Cloud IAM conditions (AWS/Azure); Zero Trust architectures that need real-time context.

Try it: a decision under RBAC
Role:
Resource:
ALLOW The Employee role may read the public wiki — general access for all staff.
DENY The Employee role has no permission on the payroll file.
ALLOW The Manager role can read the public wiki too.
ALLOW The Manager role can read and approve payroll for their team.
ALLOW The Auditor role can read the public wiki.
ALLOW (read-only) The Auditor role can inspect payroll for compliance, but cannot approve it.

Change the role or resource: the decision depends on the active role, not the person — that's RBAC's central idea.

DAC

Discretionary access control: the owner decides

  • The owner of a resource decides how it's shared (read/write to others).
  • Decentralized and identity-based; enforced with ACLs.
Example

You own a Google Doc and choose who to share it with: that's DAC.

DAC in practice

A concrete case: Linux file permissions

$ ls -l nomina.xlsx -rwxr-xr-x 1 ana finanzas 8192 Jun 9 10:14 nomina.xlsx

The 9 bits after the file-type read as three blocks: owner, group, world — exactly the subject vocabulary from the matrix.

rwxOwner (ana)read+write+exec = 7
r-xGroup (finanzas)read+exec = 5
r-xWorld (others)read+exec = 5
Tighten it

chmod 750 nomina.xlsx → rwxr-x---: the owner keeps everything (7), the group reads and executes (5), and the world loses all access (0). Each octal digit is the 4+2+1 sum of read/write/execute.

The limits of DAC

Two serious problems with DAC

🔁

No control of information flow

You can't stop the person you shared with from re-sharing it. Once it's out, it's out.

🏢

The user shouldn't always decide

In many organizations the employer mandates how sensitive data is shared — not the individual.

Discuss

Think of forwarding a confidential email: the sender lost control. How do MAC or DRM try to fix this? That leads us to the next model.

MAC

Mandatory access control: the organization decides

Subjectwith a clearance
⟷
Objectwith a classification

The system compares the subject's clearance with the object's classification. The individual can't override it.

Example

A hospital owns patient records and limits sharing — sometimes by law: HIPAA (US) or NOM-024-SSA3-2012 (Mexico). Note: these laws are regulatory drivers of MAC-style policy (the organization, not the user, decides) — they are not themselves the multilevel label mechanism of classic MAC.

Classic MAC · confidentiality

Bell-LaPadula: "no read up, no write down"

Top Secret level 4
Secret level 3
Confidential level 2
Unclassified level 1
Simple security property — "no read up" A subject can't read an object above its clearance.
Star (★) property — "no write down" A subject can't write to an object below its level (stops leaks downward).
Discuss

"No write down" feels odd — why forbid a highly-cleared person from writing to a public document? Because they could copy secrets into a level where anyone may read them.

The dual · integrity

Biba: the mirror of Bell-LaPadula

🔒

Bell-LaPadula — confidentiality

No read up · no write down. Keeps secrets from leaking downward.

🛡️

Biba — integrity

No read down · no write up. Keeps trusted data from being polluted from below.

Debate

Why do confidentiality and integrity point in opposite directions? Hint: one fears leaking down; the other fears polluting up.

Mini-quiz

Under Bell-LaPadula, can a Secret-cleared user READ a Top-Secret file? Can they WRITE to one? Read: no (that's reading up). Write: yes (writing up is allowed — the secret moves into a more-protected level, it doesn't leak).

RBAC

Role-based access control

Usersauthenticate
→
Rolesactivate one or more
→
Permissionsattached to the role

Access is based on job function: payroll manager, project member… Rights attach to roles, not people.

Analogy

Like job badges: the "nurse" role opens certain doors. People are simply added to or removed from the role.

Why RBAC is popular

RBAC benefits

  • The policy doesn't change when a person with a role leaves — you just remove them from the role.
  • A new employee simply activates the role they need.
  • It embodies least privilege: each role sees only a subset; switching roles grants other resources.
  • SELinux supports RBAC.
In industry

Microsoft retired Azure "classic" administrator roles on 31 August 2024 and asks customers to move to Azure RBAC — RBAC isn't theory, it's the cloud standard.

◆ Quick question

RBAC… a kind of MAC?

RBAC is often used in commercial settings. It's considered an example of MAC because…

1

Permissions are associated only with roles, not users.

2

Only the company can decide the roles of its employees.

Answer

2. What makes RBAC "mandatory" is that the organization — not the user — decides the roles and their permissions. That lack of user discretion is the hallmark of MAC.

More precisely: RBAC is non-discretionary: it can implement either DAC or MAC, and is not the multilevel-label MAC of Bell-LaPadula. (Ferraiolo & Kuhn, 1992 / NIST RBAC.)

Advanced RBAC

Role hierarchy and separation of duties

🪜

Role hierarchy

A senior role inherits the permissions of junior roles. "Chief physician" gets everything "physician" has, plus extras — no list is rewritten.

⚠️

Role explosion

When you mint a separate role for every permission combination, the role count grows without limit and becomes as unmanageable as the original matrix.

Separation of duties

Key constraint: the person who REQUESTS a payment cannot also APPROVE it. Two mutually exclusive roles a single user can't hold at once — so no lone individual can commit fraud unaided.

ABAC

Attribute-based access control

👤

Subject attributes

Department, job, seniority…

📄

Object attributes

Classification, owner, type…

🌐

Environment conditions

Time, location, device, network…

How it works

Centralized: a policy compares subject + object + environment attributes. Written in a readable language: XACML. E.g., "only from your office, during work hours, on your assigned device."

ABAC, concretely

An ABAC policy reads almost like a sentence

PERMIT # conceder acceso solo si todo se cumple if subject.department == resource.department and environment.time ∈ 09:00–18:00 and device.managed == true

A single rule replaces thousands of matrix rows: it combines subject, resource, and environment attributes, and is evaluated on every request.

Standards

The reference guidance is NIST SP 800-162 (Guide to ABAC). The standard policy language is XACML; cloud providers express it as policy conditions (e.g. the Condition block in AWS IAM).

ABAC · pros & cons

ABAC: powerful, but demanding

Pros

• Scalable.
• Real-time, context-aware.
• Segregation of duty: different people manage attributes and policy.

Cons

• Relatively new.
• Requires socialization and buy-in.
• Needs organizational change to manage attributes and policy.

Source: Gartner, IAM Summit 2013 — predicted 70% of businesses would use ABAC by 2020; the prediction was not met. Treat it as historical.

Discuss

There's no single winner. Which would you pick for a small school? A hospital? A global cloud company?

The common thread

It all comes back to least privilege

Whatever the model, the goal is the same: give each subject only the access they need — no more, no less.

Reflect

Recall the design principles from week 1: economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation. Access control is where they come to life.

Closing the provocation

Back to "almost all security is access control": access control governs who may do what; encryption protects data in transit and at rest even if someone gets hold of it; and availability is a separate leg of the CIA triad. Access control is the core — not the whole.

2024–2026 practice

Zero Trust: authorize on every request

NIST SP 800-207 describes a model where no one is trusted just for being "inside the network": every request is dynamically authorized. It's our reference monitor, split into three components.

PEP — Policy Enforcement Pointintercepts the request and applies the decision (≈ enforcement)
→
PDP — Policy Decision Pointevaluates policy and decides allow/deny (≈ policy)
→
PIP — Policy Information Pointsupplies attributes: identity, device, risk
Connection

The PDP/PEP is exactly our policy + enforcement framing and the reference monitor. The natural policy engine is ABAC (it decides on attributes). Google BeyondCorp popularized it; today it's written as policy-as-code with OPA (Open Policy Agent). (NIST SP 800-207, 2020; 800-207A, 2023.)

Summary

Access control, in one slide

  • A fundamental requirement whenever resources need protecting.
  • The access control matrix captures who can access what, and how.
  • ACLs and capabilities are two ways to implement it (with different trade-offs).
  • DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC govern the policy — getting it right is hard.
