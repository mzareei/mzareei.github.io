Part 1 — Security Protocols & Kerberos

Mutual authentication, key exchange, and the Kerberos ticket system.

Why protocols?

Alice and Bob want to communicate securely over the Internet

To do that, they need to agree on three things:

🪪

(Mutually) authenticate

Each party must prove to the other that they are who they claim to be.

🔑

Establish & exchange keys

Agree on a shared secret key to encrypt the conversation.

⚙️

Agree on algorithms

Settle on the cryptographic operations and algorithms to use.

Building blocks

Protocols combine pieces you already know: public-key (asymmetric) algorithms, secret-key (symmetric) algorithms, and hash functions — the topic of Part 2.

Mutual authentication: shared secret

Challenge–response with a shared key

Alice and Bob already share a secret key KAB. Neither sends the key; each proves they know it by solving a challenge only the key-holder can solve.

A nonce (R₁, R₂) is "a fresh, random question that only the real Alice can answer." It is used exactly once and never repeats.
nonce = "number used once"
The exchange, step by step

Mutual authentication with a shared secret

Alice
KAB
Bob
KAB
1.I'm Alice
2.R1
3.E[KAB, R1]
4.R2
5.E[KAB, R2]
Logic

Bob throws challenge R₁; only someone who knows KAB can return E[KAB, R₁]. Then Alice challenges Bob with R₂. At the end, both have proven they hold the key.

The nonce must be unpredictable

Why must R₁ and R₂ be large & random?

  • R₁ and R₂ must not be easily repeatable or predictable.
  • If they were, the adversary Trudy can record and replay a challenge and/or its response to impersonate Alice or Bob.
  • Fix: use large random values (e.g. 128 bits), impossible to guess or to have seen before.
  • KAB must be protected at both endpoints (Alice and Bob) — it is the root of all the security.
Analogy

If the "secret question" were always the same, anyone who eavesdrops once knows the answer forever. A fresh nonce gives every conversation its own brand-new question.

Simplified version (3 messages)

Mutual authentication: simplified

Alice
KAB
Bob
KAB
1.I'm Alice, R2
2.R1, E[KAB, R2]
3.E[KAB, R1]
Idea

To save messages, Alice sends her challenge R₂ together with "I'm Alice," and Bob replies with his challenge R₁ and the answer to R₂ in one message. Three messages instead of five — but beware, this optimization opens the door to an attack.

The reflection attack

Can Trudy authenticate as Alice without the key?

Trudy does not know KAB. But the simplified protocol lets her trick Bob into handing her the answer she needs. The trick: use Bob himself as an encryption oracle.

Key idea

Trudy does not break the cryptography. She asks Bob to encrypt exactly what she cannot encrypt, then "reflects" that answer back to him. That is why it is called a reflection attack.

Connection 1 — Trudy gets stuck

Reflection attack: step 1

Trudy (pretending to be Alice)
Bob
KAB
1.I'm Alice, R2
2.R1, E[KAB, R2]
Trudy's problem

Bob now demands step 3: E[KAB, R1]. But Trudy has no key, so she cannot compute it. Connection 1 is paused…

Connection 2 — a second channel

Reflection attack: step 2

Without closing connection 1, Trudy opens a second connection to Bob — and asks Bob to encrypt the very R₁ value she got stuck on.

Trudy (again "Alice")
Bob
KAB
1.I'm Alice, R1
2.R3, E[KAB, R1]
Bob helps!

Bob helpfully returns E[KAB, R1] — exactly the value Trudy needed for connection 1. Trudy now has her answer.

Reflect the answer back

Reflection attack: step 3 (completed)

Trudy returns to connection 1 and delivers the E[KAB, R1] Bob just gave her. Bob verifies it… and believes he is talking to Alice.

Trudy
Bob
KAB
conn 1, 3.E[KAB, R1]
Impersonation done

Trudy authenticated as Alice without knowing the key. The flaw: Bob was willing to encrypt the same kind of challenge for anyone. The "reflection" turned Bob into his own enemy.

Fix 1

Different keys for initiator and responder

  • Use KAB1 when Alice initiates and KAB2 when Bob responds.
  • Now Trudy cannot get Bob to encrypt using Alice's key — the connection-2 answer is encrypted with the "wrong" key and is useless for connection 1.
Alice
KAB1
Bob
KAB2
1.I'm Alice, R1
2.R2, E[KAB2, R1]
3.E[KAB1, R2]
Fix 2

Different challenge types per role

Initiator → even numbers

Alice (initiator) only issues and accepts even challenges.

Responder → odd numbers

Bob (responder) only issues and accepts odd challenges.

Why it works

The challenge Trudy gets from Bob in connection 2 has "the wrong parity" to slip into connection 1. The asymmetry between roles breaks the reflection.

General lesson

Protocol-design lesson: never let a message be valid in both directions. Symmetry is convenient for the programmer but a gift to the attacker.

No pre-shared secret

Mutual authentication with public keys

Each party has a key pair (PU, PR). The challenge is encrypted with the other's public key; only their private key can decrypt it.

Alice
PUA · PRA
Bob
PUB · PRB
1.I'm Alice, E[PUB, R2]
2.R2, E[PUA, R1]
3.R1
Upside

No pre-shared secret is needed — but now you must trust that PUB really belongs to Bob. Certificates solve that problem (next slides).

Session keys

Why a fresh key for each session?

  • Authenticate first, then establish a new session key for that conversation.
  • Establish a shared key for the session even if a shared secret key already exists.
  • The long-term secret key is called the master key, possibly derived from a password.
  • The master key is used to authenticate and establish a new session key — not to encrypt traffic directly.
Why it matters

If a session key leaks, only that conversation is compromised, not the entire history. Limiting use of the master key limits its exposure — this idea reappears in Kerberos and in TLS 1.3's forward secrecy.

Via master key

Establishing a session key with the master key

Alice
KAB
Bob
KAB
1.I'm Alice
2.R
3.E[KAB, R]
4.E[f(KAB), R]
Derived key

The new session key is derived from the master key via a function f(KAB). Both sides can compute it, but an eavesdropper cannot.

Via public keys

Session key with public-key crypto

Encrypt the new key

Alice → Bob:
E(PRA, E(PUB, K))

Alice encrypts the new key K with Bob's public key (confidentiality), then signs with her private key (authenticity). Only Bob can open it and he knows it came from Alice.

Diffie–Hellman with signing

Alice → Bob: E(PRA, YA)
Bob → Alice: E(PRB, YB)

Each party signs their Diffie–Hellman public contribution Y. The signature blocks man-in-the-middle; DH produces a fresh shared secret.

Today

This idea — ephemeral DH + signatures/certificates — is exactly the heart of the TLS 1.3 handshake (ECDHE + certificates). We return to it in the wrap-up.

Whose public key is this?

Exchanging public-key certificates

A trusted Certificate Authority (CA) signs a certificate that binds a name/identity to a public key. Anyone can verify the CA's signature.

CA = E(PRCA, TA ‖ IDA ‖ PUA)
CB = E(PRCA, TB ‖ IDB ‖ PUB)
  • (1) Alice sends her certificate CA to Bob; (2) Bob sends CB to Alice.
  • Each verifies the CA's signature and extracts the other's authentic public key. T is a timestamp (expiry).
On the real web

This is exactly what HTTPS does: your browser trusts a set of root CAs and validates the site's certificate before showing the padlock.

The scaling problem

Key Distribution Center (KDC)

  • Shared master keys do not scale: each communicating pair needs to share its own master key.
  • With n users you would need n(n−1)/2 keys — for 1000 users, ~500,000 keys.
  • Fix: each user shares just one master key with a trusted KDC, which hands out session keys on demand.
n(n−1)/2keys without KDC
nkeys with KDC
Analogy

The KDC is a "trusted travel agent" issuing tickets. You do not need to know every hotel in advance; you trust the agent, and the agent connects you.

The KDC hands out a session key

KDC flow

KA, KB are master keys Alice and Bob share with the KDC; KS is the session key the KDC generates.

Alice
KDC
Bob
1.Request, N1
2.E(KA, KS ‖ N1 ‖ E(KB, KS ‖ "Alice"))
3.Ticket to Bob: E(KB, KS ‖ "Alice")
4.E(KS, N2 ‖ "Bob")
5.E(KS, f(N2))
Idea

The KDC gives KS to Alice wrapped in her key, and includes a "ticket" only Bob can open. Alice never sees Bob's key; the ticket travels through Alice but only Bob decrypts it.

Kerberos

Authentication & access control in a network

  • Every principal (user or service) has a master (secret) key.
  • A human user's master key is derived from their password.
  • Other resources (printers, servers) must have their keys configured in advance.
  • All principals' master keys are stored in the KDC database, protected/encrypted.
Name

Kerberos is the three-headed dog of Greek myth — the three "heads" are the client, the server, and the KDC. The protocol was born in MIT's Project Athena.

Tickets and the TGT

The "ticket" idea in Kerberos

🎫

Ticket

An encrypted permit proving the KDC authorized a user to use a specific service. Only the target service can open it.

🎟️

Ticket-Granting Ticket (TGT)

A "ticket to ask for tickets." Issued at login (using your password) and reused all day without re-typing your key.

Analogy

The TGT is like the hand-stamp at a theme park: you get it once at the gate (login), then show it at each ride (service) without paying again.

Step A — Login (AS exchange)

Bob logs in and gets his TGT

Bob
Bob's local host
KDC
1.<Bob, passwd>
2.AS_REQ
3–4.AS_REP: E(KB, SB ‖ TGTB)
5.host decrypts, saves SB & TGTB
Day key

The KDC creates a session key SB and the TGT TGTB = E(KKDC, "Bob" ‖ SB), encrypted with the KDC's own key. Bob's password (KB) is only used to open step 3–4 — once a day.

Step B — Request a printer ticket

Accessing the printer: getting the ticket

Bob
Bob's local host
KDC
1."lpr -P hp1"
2.TGS_REQ [hp1, TGTB, E(SB, ts)]
3–4.TGS_REP: E(SB, "Bob" ‖ KBP ‖ TP)
5.host obtains KBP & TP
KDC's job

The KDC decrypts the TGTB, verifies the authenticator (timestamp), and generates key KBP and the printer ticket TP = E(KP, "Bob" ‖ KBP). The TGT avoids re-asking for the password.

Step C — Present the ticket to the printer

Accessing the printer: using the service

Bob
Bob's local host
Printer server
1.AP_REQ [TP, E(KBP, ts)]
3.AP_REP: E(KBP, ts+1)
Mutual auth

The printer decrypts TP to get KBP and verifies the authenticator. It returns ts+1 to prove to Bob it also knows KBP — mutual authentication, with no KDC call per print job.

Why is it elegant?

Kerberos benefits

🚫🔑

Host stores no passwords

Bob's local host does not need to store passwords — it only handles temporary tickets.

🕐

Master key: once a day

The master key the user shares with the KDC is only used once a day (at login).

🛡️

Limited exposure

This limits exposure of the master key: the less it travels, the less it can be intercepted.

Alive today

Kerberos v5 is very much alive: it is the backbone of Microsoft Active Directory authentication. It is not a relic textbook protocol — you use it every time you log into a corporate Windows network.

Part 1 summary

Security protocols — the essentials

  • Secret-key and public-key based authentication, with random challenge and response (nonces).
  • Impersonation attacks like reflection, and their fixes (asymmetric keys or challenges per role).
  • Establish a session key from a pre-shared secret or public keys after authenticating; use a KDC or CA.
  • Kerberos: authentication and access control via tickets and the ticket-granting ticket.

Part 2 — Hash Functions

Properties, the birthday paradox, the SHA family, block processing, and HMAC.

What is a hash function?

Cryptography's "tamper-evident seal"

A hash function takes an input of arbitrary size and produces a fixed-size output (128–512 bits) — a fingerprint of the message.

Change a single bit of the input and the fingerprint changes completely. Like a wax seal on an envelope: if anyone opens it, it shows.

H("hello") → 2cf24dba5fb0a30e…
H("Hello") → 185f8db32271fe25…  totally different!
The four key properties

Properties of a cryptographic hash function

1 · Easy to compute

Given m, computing H(m) is fast. The output is fixed length (128–512 bits).

2 · One-way (preimage resistance)

Given H(m), there is no easy way to find m. The fingerprint does not reveal the message.

3 · Second-preimage resistance

Given m1, it is infeasible to find m2 ≠ m1 with H(m2) = H(m1). (The source called this "weak collision resistance.")

4 · Collision resistance

It is infeasible to find any pair m1 ≠ m2 with H(m1) = H(m2). (The source called this "strong collision resistance.")

Modern terminology: "weak collision resistance" = second-preimage resistance; "strong collision resistance" = collision resistance. We'll use the modern names.

What are they for?

Why hashes matter

✅

Integrity

Detect any alteration of a file or message: if the hash changes, the content changed. Unique per message.

🔐

Message authentication

Combined with a secret key (HMAC), proves the message comes from whom it claims and was not modified.

🖋️

Authenticity

Digital signatures sign the hash of the message, not the whole message — faster, and binds identity to content.

Debate

A bare hash proves integrity but not authenticity: if Trudy changes the message, she can also recompute the hash. What does a "naked" hash lack to truly authenticate? (Hint: a secret key → HMAC.)

A toy example

Simple hash function using bitwise XOR

Split the message into blocks of m bits and XOR all the blocks together. Bit i of the result is the XOR of bit i of every block:

Ci = bi1 ⊕ bi2 ⊕ … ⊕ bim
Why it's insecure

It is trivial to forge collisions: swap two blocks, or flip two bits in the same position of two blocks, and the XOR is unchanged. Fine for detecting transmission errors, never for security.

Weaknesses: the collision problem

The Pigeonhole Principle and the Birthday Paradox.

Pigeonhole Principle

Collisions are inevitable

  • Let n = number of pigeons, m = number of holes.
  • If n = m: exactly one pigeon per hole is possible.
  • If n > m: at least one hole must hold more than one pigeon — a forced collision.
🐦🐦
🐦
🐦
🐦🐦
🐦
Applied to hashes

Infinite inputs, finite outputs (2n possible values). By the pigeonhole principle, collisions always exist. Security is not about preventing them, but making them infeasible to find.

◆ Activity · The Birthday Paradox

How many people in a room?

How many people do you need in a room for a greater than 50% chance that two share a birthday? (Assume 365 possible days — the "containers.")

Answer

Just 23 people. Far fewer than intuition suggests. (And for 100% certainty you would need 366 people — that part is the pigeonhole principle.)

The math

Why only 23?

We do not compare one person against the rest; we compare all possible pairs. With 23 people there are 23·22/2 = 253 pairs — many chances to match.

sample of n people from k = 365 days
p = 1 − (k)n / kn ≈ n(n−1) / 2k
p = 0.5  when  n ≈ √k  ⟶  √365 ≈ 19–23
Key rule

Finding a collision costs about √(number of outputs). For an n-bit hash there are 2n outputs, so a collision costs ≈ 2n/2, not 2n.

Choosing the hash length

Determining hash length

Hash length Possible hash values Messages to find a collision
12121/2
42422
64264232
25622562128
Security rule

An n-bit hash gives only n/2 bits of collision resistance (because of the birthday paradox). For 128-bit security you need a ≥ 256-bit output. This is why SHA-256 is today's baseline.

Weakness takeaway

Many more pigeons than pigeonholes

  • In hashing there are always far more inputs than outputs.
  • Many different messages map to the same output — many messages share the same hash.
  • Conclusion: the longer the hash length, the less likely you are to stumble onto a collision.
Debate

If collisions always exist, why do we trust hashes? Because "existing" and "being findable in practice" are very different — 2128 operations exceed humanity's compute. Security is economic, not absolute.

Secure hash algorithms: the SHA family

SHA-1, SHA-2, SHA-3 — and which ones are broken.

A short history

  • Developed by NIST, in the Secure Hash Standard (originally 1993).
  • SHA-1 (1995): 160-bit hash. SHA-2 (2002): 256/384/512-bit, similar to SHA-1 in structure.

Critical currency update

Broken hashes: MD5 and SHA-1

MD5 — broken

  • Practical collisions since 2004/2008.
  • The Flame malware (2012) forged a Microsoft certificate by abusing an MD5 collision.
  • Never use MD5 for security.

SHA-1 — broken

  • SHAttered (Google/CWI, 2017): first real SHA-1 collision (two PDFs).
  • Chosen-prefix collisions (2019–2020) made them cheap.
  • SHA-1 is deprecated / disallowed in new systems.
Use today

Use SHA-256 or SHA-3. For speed, BLAKE2 / BLAKE3 are fast, secure modern hashes (BLAKE3 is parallelizable and very fast).

A structurally different design

SHA-3 (Keccak) and the sponge construction

  • SHA-2 shares its structure and operations with its predecessors — a concern: if SHA-1 falls, would SHA-2?
  • Because of the time needed to replace SHA-2, NIST announced a 2007 competition to produce SHA-3; Keccak won.
  • SHA-3 uses a sponge construction, structurally different from the Merkle–Damgård block-chaining of SHA-1/SHA-2.
  • Standardized by NIST in FIPS 202 (2015). It does not replace SHA-2 — it is an independent alternative with an unrelated design.
Sponge vs. M–D

The sponge "absorbs" the message into a large internal state, then "squeezes" out the output — making it immune to the length-extension attack that affects SHA-1/256.

How a hash is built, and HMAC

Block-by-block processing and keyed hashes.

Message processing

Block-by-block hashing (Merkle–Damgård)

The message is padded and split into fixed-size blocks (e.g. 1024 bits in SHA-512). Each block is mixed with the previous block's result:

How it works

Hi = F(Hi−1, Mi). The padding encodes the original message length. Each block's result (Hi) feeds the next; the final HN is the hash code.

Hash-based message authentication

HMAC — a hash with a secret key

  • Cryptographic hash functions are fast, and library code is widely available.
  • A bare hash is not enough for authentication: it does not rely on a secret key, so anyone can recompute it.
  • HMAC mixes a secret key with the message before and after hashing. It was issued as RFC 2104.
  • It is the mandatory-to-implement MAC for IPsec, and is used in TLS and SET.

Source fix: HMAC is RFC 2104, not "RFC 2014." IPsec and TLS still use it; it also appears in JWTs (HS256 = HMAC-SHA256).

HMAC structure

How HMAC(K, M) is computed

HMAC(K, M) = H( (K ⊕ opad) ‖ H( (K ⊕ ipad) ‖ M ) )
  • Inner hash: the key (XORed with ipad) is prepended to the message and hashed.
  • Outer hash: the key (XORed with opad) is prepended to the inner result and hashed again.
  • ipad and opad are two distinct padding constants; the double application protects against length-extension attacks.
Design upside

HMAC treats the hash as a "black box." If SHA-256 ever needs replacing, you just plug in a different hash function without redesigning HMAC.

Why is it secure?

HMAC security

  • Security depends on the cryptographic strength of the underlying hash function.
  • It is much harder to launch successful collision attacks on HMAC because of the secret key: the attacker cannot compute the MAC without knowing K.
  • This is why HMAC remains secure even with SHA-1 in some legacy contexts — though bare SHA-1 is broken, the attack needs the key.
Bottom line

HMAC is current and secure today: it is used in TLS, IPsec, and JWTs. Even so, for new designs prefer HMAC-SHA256 over HMAC-SHA1.

A very common mistake

Passwords ≠ general-purpose hashing

❌ Never

Don't store passwords with bare SHA-256. It is too fast: an attacker tries billions per second on a GPU.

✅ Use

Use slow, salted functions designed for passwords: bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2. Their slowness is intentional.

Links to Week 2

Recall Authentication: the speed that makes a hash good for integrity makes it bad for storing passwords. Different jobs, different tools.

Tying Part 1 and Part 2 together

The real-world successor: the TLS 1.3 handshake

The textbook protocols of Part 1 live today in TLS 1.3, the protocol that secures almost the entire web:

Key exchange

Ephemeral ECDHE — elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, with forward secrecy.

Authentication

Certificates signed by a CA (and mutual TLS, mTLS, when both sides authenticate).

Key derivation

HKDF, built on HMAC, derives the session keys — Part 1 and Part 2 united.

Synthesis

Challenge–response + session keys (Part 1) + hash functions and HMAC (Part 2) = the handshake behind every HTTPS padlock you see.

Part 2 summary

Hash functions — the essentials

  • Hash length should be at least 128 bits — but the birthday paradox means ≈2n/2 messages find a collision, so use ≥256 bits today.
  • SHA-1 (160-bit) and MD5 are broken; use SHA-256, SHA-3, or BLAKE2/3.
  • The message is processed/hashed block by block, passing the result on (Merkle–Damgård); SHA-3 uses a sponge.
  • HMAC: hash the message with a secret key → message authentication; still secure and widely used (TLS, IPsec, JWT).
