01

Why does security matter?

Motivation: value, risk, and who gets hurt.

Starting point

What do we mean by “computer security”?

“The protection afforded to an automated information system in order to attain the applicable objectives of preserving the integrity, availability, and confidentiality of information system resources — hardware, software, firmware, information/data, and telecommunications.”
— NIST Computer Security Handbook [NIST95]
Notice

The definition itself names three goals — integrity, availability, confidentiality. That’s the CIA triad, and we’ll return to it at the heart of the lecture.

The core intuition

We worry about security only when two things are true

💎

We have something of value

Data, money, access, reputation, a service people depend on.

⚠️

There’s a risk it could be harmed

Someone or something could steal, alter, or deny it.

Value + risk = a security problem. No value, or no risk → nothing to secure.

Reality check

So… who is actually safe?

Large companies, governments, hospitals, universities — all have suffered major breaches. Being big or well-funded is not the same as being secure.

Live demo

Explore the breaches together: “World’s Biggest Data Breaches & Hacks”
informationisbeautiful.net/visualizations/worlds-biggest-data-breaches-hacks

Ask the room

Recognize any of these organizations? Do you have an account with one?

Motivation: profit

Your personal data is a product on a market

Individuals store a lot of sensitive data online. If it’s stolen, criminals can profit from it — there are real black markets with going rates.

What’s soldApprox. price (USD)
Hacked email / social account$1 – $15
Stolen credit-card number$5 – $30
Full identity (“fullz”)$20 – $100+
Online-banking login (by balance)$200 – $1,000+
Medical record$70 – $250

Ranges per 2025 dark-web pricing reports; they vary widely by source and year. The point: your data has a price — and per-record, medical data has historically commanded high prices.

Motivation: scale

It’s bigger than individuals — societies run on these systems

🔌

Critical infrastructure

Smart grids rely on cyber systems. Whoever controls the grid controls community infrastructure.

🏛️

Government & business data

Proprietary and state information often lives online. Unauthorized access can be economically or politically disastrous.

🌐

Everyday dependence

Societies rely on the internet. Criminals could profit by controlling it; outages ripple everywhere.

02

The security mindset

Threats, vulnerabilities, attacks — how security people see the world.

How we understand risk

Developing a “security mindset”

To understand the risk to our information and systems, we learn to ask a fixed set of questions about threats, vulnerabilities, and attacks.

  • What is of value here?
  • What kind of risk are we talking about?
  • Where do the threats come from?
  • Who is the source of the threat?
  • What security measures can we take?
Mindset

A security person instinctively asks “how could this be abused?” — not just “does it work?”

The central model

How harm actually happens: a chain

Threat actorsomeone who wants to do harm
→
Vulnerabilitya weakness they can use
→
Exploit / Attackabusing the weakness
→
Compromisebreach / loss

Threat actors exploit vulnerabilities to launch attacks; attacks lead to compromises or security breaches.

Read it left to right — and notice you can break the chain at any link to defend.

The chain, with a concrete example

Worked example: a phishing email through the four links

Link in the chainIn this phishing example
Threat actorA cybercriminal looking to profit.
VulnerabilityHuman trust + a reused password across several sites.
Attack / exploitA credential-harvesting phishing page, linked from a fake “your bank” email.
CompromiseAccount takeover — and, thanks to the reused password, other accounts too.
Notice

The same four-link model describes both a corporate breach and a scam email in your inbox.

Who attacks us

Threat actors: who wants to do us harm online

💰

Cybercriminals

Want to profit from our sensitive data for financial gain.

✊

Hacktivists

Activists who object to something you are or something you do.

🌍

Nation-states

Countries acting for political advantage or espionage.

Discuss

Different motives → different targets and tactics. Which actor would care about a student’s laptop? About a power grid?

Where weaknesses live

Vulnerabilities are everywhere — and humans are the weak link

💽

Software

Bugs and misconfigurations in the code that runs our systems.

🛰️

Networks

Weak protocols, open ports, intercepted traffic.

🧑

People

Reused passwords, clicked links, helpful answers to the wrong person.

Hard truth

It is very hard to eliminate vulnerabilities completely. The mindset starts with threats, then vulnerabilities, whose exploitation leads to attacks, which result in compromise and breaches.

  3. Free public Wi-Fi named “Airport_Free_WiFi” with no password.

03

Case study: a real breach

We’ve built the model — now let’s test it on a real incident. Let’s walk a breach through the chain we just learned.

The incident

A real-world example: the Target breach (2013)

Target, one of the largest U.S. retailers, suffered one of the most-studied breaches in history — during the 2013 holiday shopping season.

~40Mcredit/debit cards stolen
~70Mrecords of personal info (name, address, email, phone)
Nov–Dec2013, during the holiday shopping peak

Figures widely reported in public investigations and U.S. Congressional hearings.

Apply the model

Map the Target breach onto our chain

Link in the chainWhat happened at Target
Threat actorFinancially-motivated cybercriminals, after card data to resell.
VulnerabilityStolen credentials from a third-party HVAC vendor (Fazio Mechanical) + weak network segmentation.
Attack / exploitBlackPOS-style malware on point-of-sale terminals, scraping card data from memory.
CompromiseMillions of customers’ data exfiltrated; massive financial and reputational cost (next slide).
Key lesson

The attackers came in through a trusted third party, not the front door. Your security is only as strong as your vendors’.

The real cost

The damage wasn’t just data — it was financial, legal, and human

~$202Mest. total gross breach cost (partly offset by insurance)
$18.5M2017 settlement with 47 states + D.C. (a record at the time)
~$39Msettlement with banks for card-reissue costs
−46%drop in Q4 2013 profit (net profit, year-over-year — the breach was one of several factors, including Canada-expansion losses)
And also

Both the CEO and the CIO resigned in 2014; consumer class-action lawsuits followed; and brand trust took a lasting hit.

Figures from public reports and regulatory filings; totals vary by which costs are counted.

04

What do we protect?

From threats to requirements: the CIA triad.

Putting it together

How the key concepts relate

Owners value assets; assets have vulnerabilities; threats exploit them to create risk; countermeasures reduce that risk.

From problem to goals

What should we do? Meet the CIA triad

Make threats not pay, reduce vulnerabilities, and strive to meet the security requirements of sensitive information:

C
Confidentiality

Unauthorized users cannot read the information.

I
Integrity

Unauthorized users cannot alter the information.

A
Availability

Authorized users can access the information.

CIA — not the intelligence agency.

Explore it yourself

The CIA triad, interactive — tap each goal

Confidentiality — keeping secrets secret

ProtectsOnly authorized people can read the data (e.g., end-to-end encrypted messages, access control).
BreaksA leaked password database exposes millions of accounts (information disclosure).

Integrity — data stays correct

ProtectsData changes only through authorized, verified actions (e.g., digital signatures, checksums).
BreaksAn attacker silently alters a bank balance or a grade (tampering).

Availability — usable when you need it

ProtectsAuthorized users can reach the service on time (e.g., redundancy, backups, DDoS protection).
BreaksRansomware encrypts a hospital’s files; a DDoS floods a site offline (denial of service).

Tap a vertex: see what it protects and a real example of how it breaks.

Beyond the triad

CIA isn’t everything — meet “CIAAAN”

Real systems often add more requirements on top of C, I, and A:

🪪

Authentication

Giving individuals access based on verifying their identity.

🔑

Authorization

Determining access levels to files, services, programs, data, and features.

✍️

Non-repudiation

Assurance that someone cannot deny the validity of something they did. Example: you can’t deny you authorized this bank transfer — it’s digitally signed.

How it adds up

“CIAAAN” extends the triad: the three A’s = Availability (the triad’s A) + Authentication + Authorization, and the N = Non-repudiation. We don’t double-count availability.

“CIAAAN” is our course shorthand; the standard textbook extension is the Parkerian Hexad (or simply “CIA + AAA + non-repudiation”) — don’t cite “CIAAAN” as an industry-standard term.

A useful translation

It’s often easier to think in threats than in requirements

Threat (what goes wrong)Security requirement it breaks
Information disclosureConfidentiality
Tampering with informationIntegrity
Denial of service (DoS)Availability
PhishingAuthentication
Unauthorized accessAuthorization

Remember the requirements: Confidentiality · Integrity · Availability · Authentication · Authorization · Non-repudiation.

Don’t confuse these

Phishing vs. spoofing

🎭

Spoofing — forging an identity

Forges a source or identity: the sender address, the caller ID, a website.

🎣

Phishing — manipulating a victim

Social-engineers a victim into revealing info or taking an action (click, log in, pay).

How they combine

Spoofing forges an identity; phishing manipulates a person. They frequently combine — a phishing email usually uses a spoofed sender: a “your bank” email asking you to click and log in.

Quick check

Match the threat to the requirement

Rapid fire

Which requirement does each break? — a DoS attack · an altered bank balance · a leaked password file.

Answers

availability · integrity · confidentiality.

The “C”

Confidentiality: need-to-know access

  • Access on a “need to know” basis.
  • How do we know who needs what? → access control specifies who can access what.
  • How do we know a user is who they claim? → identification & authentication.

Two faces of confidentiality

Difficult to ensure in practice…

…but easiest to assess: success is essentially binary — either the secret leaked, or it didn’t (Yes / No).

Physical analogy: “need to access/use” a computer room or a specific desktop.

The “I”

Integrity: guarding against unauthorized change

ConfidentialityIntegrity
Concerned with access to assetsConcerned with unauthorized modification of assets
Easier to measure (binary)Harder to measure — degrees of integrity, context-dependent

Your bank account

Integrity = the balance is accurate and changes only through authorized transactions.

Your medical record

Integrity = entries are correct and unaltered — wrong data can be dangerous.

Integrity can mean accuracy, precision, currency, consistency, meaningfulness, usefulness… depending on context.

The “A”

Availability: usable when you need it

Complex and context-dependent. An asset (data or service) is available when it offers:

  • Timely response to requests
  • Fair allocation of resources (no starvation)
  • Fault tolerance (no total breakdown)
  • Easy to use in the intended way
  • Controlled concurrency (concurrency & deadlock control)
  • Completed in an acceptable time
Concrete example

A DDoS attack floods a site with fake traffic until it goes down; ransomware encrypts a hospital’s files and halts care. In both, the data exists but is not available when needed — and every hour of downtime costs money or lives.

[Pfleeger & Pfleeger]

The trade-off

You must balance C, I, and A — they pull against each other

Example 1 — boost C, hurt I + A

Disconnect a computer from the internet to raise confidentiality. But availability drops, and integrity suffers from lost updates.

Example 2 — boost I, hurt C + A

Add extensive checks by many people/systems to raise integrity. But confidentiality drops (more eyes on the data) and availability drops (data locked during verification).

Takeaway

There is no “maximum security” — only the right balance for a given system and its risks.

05

What should the good guys do?

From goals to action: defense and design.

The defender’s playbook

Four jobs of defense — plus one big distinction

Preventionstop it happening
→
Detectionnotice it fast
→
Responsecontain it
→
Recoveryrestore & remediate
Key distinction

Policy (the what) — the security goals we require — vs. mechanism (the how) — the technical means that enforce them.

How to reduce vulnerabilities

Classic principles for designing secure systems

Economy of mechanism

Keep it simple — complexity is the enemy of security. e.g. keep it simple enough to audit.

Fail-safe defaults

Default to no access; grant explicitly. e.g. the firewall denies by default.

Complete mediation

Check every access, every time. e.g. check every access, every time.

Open design

Security must not depend on the design being secret. e.g. AES is public yet secure — no security through obscurity.

Least privilege

Give each part only the access it needs. e.g. your app doesn’t run as root.

Psychological acceptability

If security is too painful, people route around it. e.g. if it fights the user, they bypass it.

…and more. These date back decades and still hold up.

Lesson summary

The security mindset, in one slide

  • Cybersecurity is a huge problem for people, governments, and companies alike.
  • Our job is to raise the level of assurance of systems.
  • The mindset requires knowing: the threats, the actors and their motivations, and how they successfully attack.
  • We protect Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — balanced, never maximized.
One thing to remember

Think like an attacker to defend like a pro.
