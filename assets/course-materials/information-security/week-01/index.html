TC2007B - Week 01

Security Mindset and the CIA Triad

This lesson introduces the basic language of information security: assets, threats, vulnerabilities, attacks, risk, and the core security requirements of confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Lecture mode

Week 01

Class opening

Begin with people, not only systems.

Security is easier to understand when students first connect it to everyday experiences: valuable information, possible harm, and the practical decisions people make when risk is present.

Icebreaker 1

What one invention would you uninvent, and why?

Use the answer to surface unintended consequences of technology.

Icebreaker 2

Have you been affected by a cybersecurity incident?

Discuss only what is appropriate to share in class.

By the end of this class, students should be able to:

  • Explain why cybersecurity matters when valuable assets are exposed to risk.
  • Distinguish threats, vulnerabilities, attacks, and compromises.
  • Identify common threat actors and their motivations.
  • Use confidentiality, integrity, and availability to analyze security problems.
  • Recognize that security requirements often require tradeoffs.

Core idea

We worry about security when something valuable can be harmed.

A security mindset starts by asking what has value, how it could be harmed, who might try to harm it, and what protection is reasonable.

NIST frames computer security as protection for automated information systems so that confidentiality, integrity, and availability can be preserved for system resources.

In-class activity

Pick one everyday system: a bank app, university email, smart grid, medical record, or social network. Name one asset, one possible threat, and one security requirement that matters.

Concept map

Threat actors exploit vulnerabilities to launch attacks.

Security analysis becomes clearer when the vocabulary is separated: assets are what matter, threats are possible sources of harm, vulnerabilities are weaknesses, and attacks are actions that exploit those weaknesses.

Information, systems, services, infrastructure.

Nation-states

Security requirements

The CIA triad gives a first framework for protection goals.

Confidentiality

Unauthorized users cannot read information. A common approach is access control: specifying who can access what, supported by identification and authentication.

Useful classroom phrase: need-to-know basis.

Integrity

Integrity is concerned with unauthorized modification of assets. It is often harder to measure than confidentiality because it can depend on context and degree.

Example contexts: bank account, medical record.

Availability

A resource is available when authorized users can use it in the intended way: timely responses, fair resource allocation, fault tolerance, usability, and controlled concurrency.

Availability can apply to data, services, and infrastructure.

Extensions

CIA is essential, but it is not the whole security picture.

Authentication

Verifying that a user or system is who they claim to be.

Authorization

Determining access levels for files, services, data, and features.

Non-repudiation

Assurance that someone cannot deny the validity of an action or transaction.

Threats and requirements

Reveal the security requirement that most directly matches each threat.

Threat Requirement

Phishing versus spoofing

Phishing tricks a person into giving up sensitive information. Spoofing impersonates a trusted source and can be used as a delivery method. In short: phishing is a method of retrieval, while spoofing is a means of delivery.

Response

Good security combines requirements, mechanisms, and tradeoffs.

Confidentiality can reduce availability.

Disconnecting a computer from the internet may increase confidentiality, but availability suffers and integrity may suffer if updates are lost.

Integrity checks can affect confidentiality and availability.

Extensive verification can improve integrity, but it may expose data to more people and slow access while data is under review.

Design principles introduced in class

  • Economy of mechanism: complexity is the enemy.
  • Fail-safe defaults: the default situation is lack of access.
  • Complete mediation.
  • Open design.
  • Least privilege.
  • Psychological acceptability.

A useful distinction: policy states what should happen; mechanism describes how the system enforces it.
