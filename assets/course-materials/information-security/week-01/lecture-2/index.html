01

Ethics & law

Rules we write down vs. rules we feel.

A key distinction

How do ethics and law differ?

EthicsLaw
An individual standard of rightA societal standard, collectively agreed
No external arbiter or enforcementEnforced by courts and penalties
Varies by person, profession, and cultureThe same for everyone within the jurisdiction

Legal but unethical

Selling your users’ data to advertisers is allowed by your Terms of Service, yet betrays their trust.

Illegal but arguably ethical

A whistleblower leaks evidence of illegal mass surveillance: breaks the law, yet many defend it as a moral duty.

Key idea

“Legal” and “ethical” are not the same. Professional codes of ethics — such as those of (ISC)², IEEE, and ACM — exist to guide you when the law is silent.

A real ethical dilemma

You find a bug: coordinated or full disclosure?

Coordinated disclosure

You report privately to the vendor, give them time to patch, and publish only afterward. Protects users; relies on the vendor acting.

Full disclosure

You publish the details immediately. Pressures the vendor, but leaves users exposed until a patch exists.

Day 0private report to vendor
→
Day 1–90window to patch (typically 90 days)
→
After patchcoordinated advisory published
Discuss

If 90 days pass and the vendor ignores your report, is it ethical to publish it to protect users? Or does that put more people at risk?

No

🔴

Hint

There’s no “obvious” answer — courts have wrestled with this. Let’s look at a real case next.

Real case

hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn — is scraping illegal?

hiQ scraped public LinkedIn profiles for analytics tools (Keeper, Skill Mapper). LinkedIn responded with cease-and-desist letters.

YearWhat happened
2019Accessing public data ≠ “unauthorized access” under the U.S. CFAA. Blocking access seen as anti-competitive.
2021The case was returned for reconsideration.
2022Original ruling reaffirmed: public scraping remains outside “unauthorized access” under the CFAA.
Late 2022But there was breach of contract (scraping + fake accounts), plus liability for California common-law torts (trespass to chattels and misappropriation). Settlement: hiQ pays $500K and ceases operating on LinkedIn data.
Jurisdiction

This is U.S. law (the CFAA). Mexico has no equivalent scraping precedent — don’t assume the outcome transfers.

02

The legal framework in Mexico

Three laws every cybersecurity professional should know.

Overview

Three pillars of the Mexican framework

©️

Copyright (LFDA)

Protects intellectual property — including digital works — against unauthorized reproduction or distribution.

🛒

E-commerce law

Sets standards for secure online transactions and protects consumers’ data.

🛡️

Personal-data protection (2025 law)

Requires private entities to protect the personal data they hold — the new federal law in force since March 2025.

Ley Federal de Derechos de Autor

LFDA: copyright, applied to software

“The Ley Federal de Derechos de Autor protects the rights of authors and creators against unauthorized reproduction or distribution, including digital works.”

In software & cybersecurity it covers: copyright protection, authorship, software licensing, online infringement, and penalties.

Everyday analogy

Think of it like a lease versus a deed. Most software licenses (EULAs) give you a lease — the right to use the program — not a deed that makes you its owner. Copying it and handing it to a classmate “to help them out” is still unauthorized distribution under the LFDA, no matter how good the intention.

LFDA · interesting facts

What the LFDA means for security people

🔓

Art. 114 — security testing

Circumventing technological protection measures is not a violation when done for certain purposes — like security testing, or accessibility for people with disabilities.

🌐

Art. 114 — ISPs

Limits ISP liability for infringement on their networks — if they remove infringing content upon notice.

🏷️

Rights-management info

The LFDA protects copyright-management information (e.g., metadata identifying the author). Altering it can hide the source of pirated content.

LFDA · a concrete case

What if I share the textbook PDF?

📚

Redistributing a copyrighted PDF

Uploading the textbook PDF to a WhatsApp group or a Drive folder is unauthorized distribution of a protected work — exactly what the LFDA penalizes.

🔓

Cracking the DRM

Bypassing the DRM on an e-book or piece of software circumvents a technological protection measure. Outside the exceptions (e.g., security testing, accessibility), it can be an infringement.

Discuss

“But I bought it, it’s mine.” Does buying a copy give you the right to copy and share it? Does it change anything if you charge nothing?

LFDA · penalties

Infringement costs — measured in multiples

ArticleFine (in UMA — Unidad de Medida y Actualización)
Art. 230Author-rights infractions: 8,000–22,000 UMA (serious) and 1,500–8,000 UMA (lesser).
Art. 231Defines the commercial infractions (the list of conducts) — it does not set the fine figures.
Art. 232Imposes the commercial-infraction fines: roughly 5,000–40,000 UMA, 1,000–5,000 UMA, or 500–1,000 UMA depending on the fraction.
Art. 233Up to 50% higher for large-scale infringers (e.g., publishers and broadcasters).

In Mexico these fines are calculated in UMA (Unidad de Medida y Actualización), updated yearly — check the current UMA value.

LFDA · no prison?

Fines under the LFDA, but prison under the Criminal Code

The LFDA: fines only

The LFDA establishes only monetary fines for copyright infringement — not prison sentences.

The Criminal Code: prison

Art. 424: unauthorized reproduction/publication of a work → 6 months to 6 years. Art. 424 BIS: making/distributing/selling devices to decode encrypted satellite signals → 3 to 10 years.

In practice

Prison mainly applies in severe cases: large-scale piracy for profit.

E-commerce in Mexico

There is no single “E-commerce Law”

In Mexico, e-commerce is not governed by one law: the rules are distributed across the Código de Comercio and the Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor (LFPC).
  • The international milestone was the OECD’s Ottawa Ministerial Conference (1998) and its consumer-protection guidelines (1999) — not the start of Mexican regulation.
  • The LFPC promotes and protects consumer rights, seeking fairness and legal certainty between suppliers and consumers.
  • The Mexican cyber-consumer is protected when buying online — overseen by Profeco (the Federal Consumer Protection Agency).
Personal-data protection · 2025 law

Mexico’s new federal data-protection law (2025)

Principles & rights

Sets principles and rights for personal data held by private parties: consent, notice, access, rectification, cancellation, and opposition.

Oversight

Oversight and enforcement now sit with the Secretaría Anticorrupción y Buen Gobierno (Secretariat of Anti-Corruption and Good Governance), which monitors compliance, hears complaints, and imposes sanctions.

What changed

Mexico repealed the 2010 LFPDPPP and enacted a new federal law, in force since ~20 March 2025. The INAI was dissolved, and its functions moved to the Secretaría Anticorrupción y Buen Gobierno.

Personal data · definitions

Personal data vs. sensitive data

Personal data

Any information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person.

Sensitive data

Data affecting a person’s most intimate sphere, or whose misuse could cause discrimination or grave risk.

Examples

Sensitive data includes: health, genetic data, religion, philosophical or political views, and sexual orientation.

Personal data · sensitive data

Sensitive data demands extra protection

  • Extra protections are mandated for sensitive data.
  • It is prohibited to build databases of sensitive data without justifying explicit, legitimate purposes tied to the entity’s activity — limiting unnecessary collection.
  • There are specific security requirements: administrative, technical, and physical safeguards appropriate to the risk and sensitivity of the data.
Personal data · compliance

Breach notice, ARCO rights, and penalties

📣

Notify the breach

Breaches that significantly affect data subjects must be reported to them so they can take defensive measures.

🙋

ARCO rights

Individuals can exercise Access, Rectification, Cancellation, and Opposition over their data.

⚠️

Penalties

Administrative fines of 100 to 320,000 UMA, higher for sensitive-data breaches and repeat offenders; prison in cases of bad faith or fraud.

New in 2025

The new law now expressly covers data processors, not just controllers.

Quick check

Which law applies?

Match

(1) An online store leaks customers’ purchase histories. (2) Someone resells licensed software by copying it. (3) An unauthorized charge on an online purchase. — Which law is primary in each case?

For discussion

(1) Data protection (2025 law) · (2) Copyright (LFDA) · (3) E-commerce / consumer protection. Several can overlap — that’s the point.

Ownership & the “right to be forgotten”

Who decides what is yours?

◆ One last question

The “right to be forgotten”

In Google Spain v. AEPD (CJEU, Case C-131/12, 2014), the EU Court of Justice ruled that citizens have a “right to be forgotten”: Google must not return links to information that is “inaccurate, inadequate, irrelevant or excessive.” Which one should Google NOT return?

⚖️

A

A story about a criminal conviction that was revoked on appeal.

🩺

B

A doctor asks to remove links to news stories about botched procedures he performed.

For discussion

A. A revoked conviction is now inaccurate/irrelevant → should not be returned. The doctor’s information is of public interest and usually stays. “Forgetting” balances privacy against the public’s right to know.

Jurisdiction

This is EU law. Mexico has no right to be forgotten of equivalent scope to the EU’s — don’t assume it applies the same way here.

Let’s get philosophical

If you say “it’s mine,” who gave you that right?

Is yours always really yours? And why should I believe it’s yours?

Six “stories” explain most ownership claims

First-in-time

“I was here first.”

Possession

“Possession is nine-tenths of the law.”

Labor

“You made it, you own it.”

Attachment

“If it’s on my land, it’s my property.”

Self-ownership

“My body, my choice.”

“Family passes it down.”

04

Privacy

What it is, what threatens it, and how to protect it.

Before we continue…

What is privacy?

A person’s ability to control how data about them can be collected, used, and shared by someone else.
Key

The key word is control — not “secrecy.” Privacy is deciding who knows what about you.

Old worry, new scale

Privacy isn’t a new problem — but the scale is

People have always worried about what others — friends, enemies, governments — might know about what they do.

What changed

The scale and magnitude at which information about us can be collected — and the ways it can be used, shared, or sold.

Draw the line

What’s private… and what isn’t?

Usually private

  • Finances: statements, cards, banking records
  • Health/medical conditions; biometrics (fingerprints)
  • Legal matters; political beliefs
  • Web-browsing habits; communications (emails, calls)
  • Past history (right to be forgotten)

Not private? (discuss)

  • Where I live? My citizenship?
  • That I’m registered to vote?
  • My salary? (… really?)
Note

The line depends on context and culture — hence all the question marks.

Not only individuals

Privacy matters for organizations too

Universities, hospitals, and charities require privacy — they must protect the data of the people they serve or employ.

Think

A leak at a hospital or university harms thousands of people who never chose to take that risk.

Analogy

It’s like a bank vault: no one outside “owns” the vault, but everyone — account holders, employees, regulators — trusts whoever runs it to keep it locked. A university or hospital plays that same custodian role for the data it collects.

Threats

How privacy gets eroded

📱

Social media

We know your friends — and a lot more from what you post.

🍪

Web tracking (cookies)

Your activity is followed across sites.

📍

Location-aware apps

They know where you’ve been.

🎟️

Sometimes we opt in

Store loyalty cards: we trade data for discounts.

Privacy-enhancing technologies

Tor: hiding where you come from

Alice doesn’t want the web service to know she is accessing it. Tor routes her traffic through several relays, so traffic analysis can’t reveal her origin.

Alicereal origin (hidden)
→
Tor relaysseveral encrypted hops
→
Web serviceonly sees the last relay
Idea

No single relay knows both who you are and what you’re asking for — that separation is what protects your identity.

Limits

The exit node sees your unencrypted traffic; traffic-correlation attacks can deanonymize you; and Tor ≠ anonymity once you log into accounts. A VPN just shifts trust to one provider, and end-to-end encryption hides content but not who is talking to whom.

◆ Activity · Sort the scenario

Authorized or not? Sort five real-world scenarios

For each scenario, decide: is this an authorized (legal) action, or an unauthorized (illegal) one? Tap your answer, then check the reasoning.

Scenario 1 / 5

You port-scan a company’s servers without permission.

Verdict: unauthorized / illegal

No authorization — scanning systems you don’t own or have explicit permission to test is unauthorized access, regardless of intent.

You find a bug and report it privately to the vendor.

Verdict: authorized / legal

Private, good-faith disclosure to the vendor is the responsible-disclosure norm — it respects their chance to fix it before anyone else finds out.

You access data you can see but were never authorized to view.

Verdict: unauthorized / illegal

Being able to see it isn’t the same as being in authorized scope to access it — visibility is often just a misconfiguration, not permission.

You test your own website for vulnerabilities.

Verdict: authorized / legal

You own the system, so you’re the one who can grant authorization to test it — this is exactly the kind of security testing laws like the LFDA carve out as an exception.

You share a colleague’s password “just to help.”

Verdict: unauthorized / illegal

Good intentions don’t create consent — the account owner never agreed to it, and it breaks accountability (who did what?) for everyone who uses that login.

The pattern

Almost this whole unit boils down to four words: authorization, consent, scope, and disclosure. If one is missing, you’re probably on the wrong side of the line.

Key takeaways

Legal & ethical, in one slide

  • Ethics is an individual standard; law is societal and enforced — they often reinforce each other.
  • In Mexico: LFDA (copyright), e-commerce split across the Código de Comercio and the LFPC / Profeco, and the 2025 personal-data law form the core framework.
  • Data ownership is philosophically slippery — which is why regulation matters.
  • Privacy is control, not secrecy: who collects, uses, and shares your data.
