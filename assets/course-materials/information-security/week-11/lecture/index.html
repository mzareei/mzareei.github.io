Intrusion Detection (IDS / IPS)

Prevention isn't always possible — so we must also detect. And detecting well is harder than it looks.

The context: defense in depth

Prevent · Detect · Survive

🧱PreventFirewalls, encryption, strong auth — stop the attack at the door.

Key idea

Intrusion detection is the second line: it assumes prevention is never perfect. If the firewall (Week 10) is the wall, the IDS is the guard watching inside.

01

What is an intrusion, and how do we detect it?

Definitions, examples, and the assumptions that make detection possible.

Definitions

Security intrusion & intrusion detection

Security intrusion: an unauthorized act of bypassing the security mechanisms of a system.
Intrusion detection: a hardware or software function that gathers and analyzes information from various areas within a computer or network to identify possible security intrusions.
Nuance

"Bypassing mechanisms" includes abuse of legitimate credentials. An IDS doesn't only look for malicious code — it looks for unauthorized behavior, even from a valid account.

What counts as an intrusion?

Examples of intrusion

  • Remote root compromise of an e-mail server.
  • Web server defacement.
  • Guessing / cracking passwords.
  • Copying databases of credit-card numbers.
  • Viewing sensitive data without authorization.
  • Running a packet sniffer.
  • Distributing pirated software.
  • Using an unsecured modem to reach the internal network.
  • Impersonating an executive to get information.
  • Using an unattended workstation.
Notice

Some are technical (remote root, sniffer); others are human (impersonation, unattended workstation). Detection must span network, host, and behavior.

What is an IDS effective against?

Effectiveness against different attack types

✅

Effective

Known, less-sophisticated attacks: Internet background noise, mass scans, signatured malware.

⚠️

Partial

Sophisticated, targeted attacks: only detectable if they leave observable anomalous evidence.

❌

Not effective

New zero-day exploits: no signature, no history — signature detection is blind to them.

Debate

If an IDS can't catch zero-days, what's the point? Hint: most real breaches don't use zero-days — they reuse known techniques. Catching the known still matters enormously.

The IDS doesn't work alone

Defense-in-depth strategies that surround the IDS

  • Encryption of data at rest and in transit.
  • Detailed audit trails — the raw material of host-based detection.
  • Strong authentication and authorization controls.
  • Active management of operating systems (patching, hardening).
  • Application security (see Week 3).
Connection

The IDS feeds on these other layers: no good audit logs means no host-based detection; no network segmentation means no good vantage point.

The two founding assumptions

Elements of intrusion detection

Assumption 1

System activities are observable — packets, system calls, accesses, and sessions can be recorded.

Assumption 2

Normal and intrusive activities have distinct evidence — otherwise they'd be indistinguishable and detection would be impossible.

What if they fail?

When assumption 2 weakens — when an attack "looks" almost like normal traffic — the IDS either misses it (false negative) or flags the normal as an attack (false positive). That tension runs through the whole lecture.

Two perspectives

Components of an intrusion detection system

Algorithmic perspective

  • Features: capture the evidence of intrusion.
  • Models: piece the evidence together to decide.

System-architecture perspective

  • Audit data processor
  • Knowledge base
  • Decision engine
  • Alarm generation and responses

02

Three axes of classification

Modeling · Deployment · Development & maintenance.

How to classify an IDS

Intrusion detection approaches

🧪

Modeling & analysis

Misuse detection (a.k.a. signature-based) vs. anomaly detection.

📍

Deployment

Host-based (HIDS) vs. network-based (NIDS).

🛠️

Development & maintenance

Hand-coding of "expert knowledge" vs. learning from data.

Note

These axes are independent: a network IDS can use signatures and hand-coded rules (e.g. Snort), or learned anomalies; a host IDS can do either. Real tools mix the axes.

The two core philosophies

Anomaly vs. signature detection

Anomaly detectionMisuse / signature detection
Models the normal; flags what deviates.Models the malicious; flags what matches.
Collects legitimate-user behavior over time; compares current behavior to that profile.Uses known malicious data patterns or attack rules, compared against current behavior.
Can detect novel attacks… at the cost of many false alarms.Only identifies known attacks for which it has a pattern or rule.
Analogy

Signatures = "wanted posters": they only catch already-known criminals. Anomaly = your bank's "this charge is unusual for you" alert: it flags the unusual, though it sometimes misfires on a legitimate purchase.

03

Anomaly detection

Learn what "normal" is — and distrust everything else.

Three classification families

How "normal" is modeled

Statistical

Analyzes observed behavior using captured sensor data to build a statistical profile of the metrics.

Knowledge-based

An expert system classifies observed behavior according to a set of rules that model legitimate behavior.

Machine-learning

Automatically determines a classification model from the training data using data-mining techniques.

In common

All three learn from legitimate data to judge the new. The difference is who defines the model: statistics, a human expert, or the algorithm.

Statistical approach

Statistical profiles: simple but limited

Characteristics: uses captured sensor data (univariate models); multivariate models incorporating the time and order of events.

Advantages

  • Relative simplicity.
  • Low computation cost.
  • No prior assumptions about expected behavior.

Disadvantages

  • Difficulty selecting suitable metrics.
  • Not all behaviors can be modeled this way.
Knowledge-based approach

Expert rules that characterize the normal

Classifies observed data using a set of rules developed during training to characterize the data into distinct classes.

Advantages

  • Robust.
  • Flexible.

Disadvantages

  • Difficulty and time required to develop knowledge from the data.
  • Human experts must assist with the process.
Machine-learning approach

Learned models: powerful but costly

Uses data-mining techniques to develop a model that classifies data as normal or anomalous.

Advantages

  • Flexibility.
  • Adaptability.
  • Captures interdependencies between observed metrics.

Disadvantages

  • Depends on assumptions about accepted behavior.
  • High false-alarm rate.
  • High resource cost; significant time and computation.
Hold this thought

"High false-alarm rate" sounds like a footnote — but it is the operational problem of IDS. We'll return to it with the base-rate fallacy.

The menu of techniques

Machine-learning techniques for detection

Bayesian networks

Encode probabilistic relationships among observed metrics.

Markov models

Build a model with sets of states and transitions.

Neural networks

Simulate the brain with neurons and synapses between them.

Fuzzy logic

Approximate reasoning that accommodates uncertainty.

Genetic algorithms

Inspired by evolutionary biology (inheritance, mutation, selection) to evolve classification rules.

Clustering & outliers

Group data into clusters; points that fit none are outliers (anomalies).

The Achilles' heel

Limitations of anomaly detection

They are generally trained on legitimate data only. This limits the effectiveness of some of the techniques.
  • If "normal" shifts (new software, new habits), the model raises false alarms until retrained — concept drift.
  • An attacker can poison the training: act "normal" while the system learns, normalizing their attack.
  • Setting the normal-vs-anomalous threshold is a direct trade-off between false positives and false negatives.
04

Misuse / signature detection

The other half: hunt for the known bad.

Three steps

How signature detection spots an intrusion

1 · Observeevents in the system
→
2 · Applya set of patterns or rules to the data
→
3 · Decidewhether the data is intrusive or normal
Real tools

Snort and Suricata are signature NIDS: each rule describes a malicious packet pattern (the modern "wanted poster"). Zeek (formerly Bro) goes further: it logs and analyzes network behavior for flexible detection.

A Snort signature, and the trade-off

What a signature looks like — and what it costs

# Snort rule — alert on a known SQLi probe / regla Snort
alert tcp any any -> $HOME_NET 80 ( content:"' OR '1'='1"; msg:"SQLi attempt"; sid:1000001; )

Advantages

  • Low cost in time and resources.
  • Wide acceptance and maturity.
  • Few false alarms: a signature match is almost certainly an attack.

Disadvantages

  • Significant effort to identify and review new malware and create signatures.
  • Cannot detect zero-day attacks — no signature, no detection.
05

Monitoring networks and hosts

Where the sensor sits — and what it can do.

Passive monitoring

An IDS, by default, only observes

  • An IDS performs passive monitoring: it records and analyzes data about system and network activity.
  • Only if the IDS raises an alert and the response policy dictates intervention are activities affected.

NIDS

Network-based: watches traffic on a segment. Sees many hosts at once, but not encrypted payloads or what happens on the host itself.

HIDS

Host-based: watches logs, system calls, and files on one machine. Sees decrypted activity, but only for that host.

Two ways to wire the sensor

Passive vs. inline sensors

Network traffic →
Passive sensor
Gets a copy (mirror port / TAP). Real traffic does NOT pass through it.
Inline sensor
Monitored traffic must pass through it; it can block an attack on detection.

Passive: more efficient, no single point of failure in the traffic path. Monitoring interface has no IP, runs in promiscuous mode. But it only detects.

Inline: does detection and prevention. Achieved by combining NIDS logic with a firewall or LAN switch (no extra hardware), or with a stand-alone inline NIDS sensor.

Link to Week 10

Firewall vs. network IDS

FirewallNetwork IDS
Active filtering: decides what passes.Passive monitoring: observes and alerts.
Fail-close: if it fails, default is to block (prioritizes security).Fail-open: if it fails, traffic still flows (prioritizes availability).
Decides by policy rules (ports, IPs, state).Decides by content and behavior analysis.
Debate

The IPS (coming up) sits inline like a firewall but decides like an IDS. Should it then fail-close or fail-open? What do you prioritize: security or availability?

How "good" is an IDS, really?

Performance metrics — and the trap that ruins many IDS.

The four cells

Confusion matrix: true/false, positive/negative

Reality: attackReality: normal
IDS says: alarm True positive (TP)
attack caught		 False positive (FP)
false alarm
IDS says: nothing False negative (FN)
attack missed		 True negative (TN)
correct silence
Debate

Which is worse for a SOC: a false negative (missing an attack) or 10,000 false positives (drowning in alarms)? There's no single answer — it depends on the cost of each error.

From cells to metrics

The metrics that actually matter

Recall / detection rateTP / (TP + FN)
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)
False-alarm rateFP / (FP + TN)
  • Recall (detection rate): of all real attacks, how many did I catch? Low recall = attacks slip through.
  • Precision: of all my alarms, how many were real attacks? Low precision = the analyst wastes time.
  • Accuracy (overall) is misleading when one class is very rare — exactly the case for intrusions. Hence the base-rate fallacy, next.
Central tension

Lowering the threshold raises recall but lowers precision (more false alarms). Raising it does the reverse. You cannot maximize both.

The ROC curve

ROC: the trade-off, visualized

  • X-axis: false-positive rate (FP / negatives).
  • Y-axis: true-positive rate = recall.
  • The dashed diagonal = random guessing.
  • The more the blue curve bows to the top-left, the better the detector (AUC → 1).
  • The red dot is an operating point: the chosen threshold. Moving it slides along the curve — always trading detection for false alarms.
The statistical trap

The base-rate fallacy

When attacks are extremely rare, even a very accurate IDS produces overwhelmingly more false alarms than true ones — because there is vastly more normal traffic than attacks.
Medical intuition

A test for a rare disease with 99% accuracy: if you test positive, you're most likely healthy. Why? Because healthy people are so numerous that their few false positives outnumber the few truly sick. The IDS suffers exactly the same.

Let's run the numbers

A concrete example, line by line

# 1,000,000 sessions/day; only 100 are attacks (0.01%)
# IDS: 99% detection rate, 1% false-alarm rate

True positives = 99% × 100 = 99 attacks caught
False positives = 1% × 999,900 = 9,999 false alarms

Precision = 99 / (99 + 9,999) = ≈ 0.98% ⟵ !!
The lesson

Out of every 100 alarms, only ~1 is real. The analyst sifts 99 false alarms for every true attack. A "99% accurate" IDS can be operationally useless if it generates more noise than a human team can triage. This is why a low false-alarm rate matters more than raw accuracy.

Why this still hurts in 2025

AI, anomalies, and the problem that won't die

  • ML/AI detection is now mainstream — but anomaly detection still collides with the base-rate fallacy: many alarms, few real.
  • This is why "high false-alarm rate" (the ML slide) is the central operational pain, not a footnote.
  • ML detectors are also vulnerable to adversarial evasion: inputs crafted so the model labels them as normal.
The modern SOC

SIEM (Splunk, Elastic, Microsoft Sentinel) aggregates and correlates IDS and host logs to cut noise; SOAR automates response; EDR/XDR extends detection to the endpoint and NDR to the network. The IDS is one feed within this ecosystem.

07

How attackers fool an IDS

Insertion, evasion, and denial of service against the detector.

Ptacek & Newsham, 1998

Insertion and evasion attacks

Insertion

The IDS accepts a packet the end host will reject. The attacker "inserts" junk only the IDS sees, polluting its view of the stream and breaking signature matching.

# attacker sends:
a X t X t a c k
# IDS sees "aXtXtack" — no match
# host drops the X's → "attack"

Evasion

The opposite: the end host accepts a packet the IDS discards. Part of the attack slips "under the radar" of the IDS but reaches the victim intact.

# IDS drops a fragment it thinks invalid
a t t [seen by host only] a c k
# IDS sees "att...ack"? → no match
# host reassembles → "attack"
Root cause

The IDS and host can interpret the same bytes differently (TTL, IP fragmentation, checksums, TCP reassembly). The defense: traffic normalization — make the IDS see exactly what the host will see.

Third vector: exhaust the detector

Denial of service against the IDS

  • Overload: flood the IDS so it can't process everything; if it's passive and fail-open, the real attack passes while it's saturated.
  • State exhaustion: open millions of connections to fill the sensor's reassembly/state tables.
  • Alarm storm: deliberately trigger thousands of false positives to hide the real attack in the noise — the base-rate fallacy weaponized.
Link to Week 9

Mapping detections to MITRE ATT&CK helps prioritize: instead of drowning in isolated alarms, the analyst asks "which adversary technique is this?" and correlates attack stages (recall the malware of Week 9).

08

Honeypots

Don't wait for the attacker — lure them into a trap.

The decoy

What is a honeypot, and what is it for?

Honeypots are decoy systems designed to lure attackers away from critical systems.
🪤

Divert

Steer the attacker away from real assets.

🔬

Collect

Gather intelligence on the attacker's tactics.

⏳

Detain

Keep them engaged long enough for admins to respond.

Analogy

A honeypot is a decoy safe: it looks valuable, is stuffed with fake cash, and the instant someone touches it the alarm rings — because no legitimate person would have any reason to open it.

Why a honeypot barely false-alarms

Properties of a honeypot

  • Filled with fabricated information.
  • Any access triggers monitors and event loggers.
  • An attack against it is made to seem successful (to retain the attacker).
  • It has no production value; there is no legitimate reason to access it.
  • So any attempt to communicate with it is almost certainly a probe, scan, or attack.
  • If the honeypot initiates outbound traffic, the system is most likely compromised.
The big win

Because no legitimate user touches it, the honeypot sidesteps the base-rate fallacy: nearly every alarm is a true positive. It's the opposite of a noisy NIDS.

Two levels of realism

Low- vs. high-interaction honeypots

Low-interaction

  • Emulates services well enough for a realistic initial interaction, without running them for real.
  • A less realistic target.
  • Often sufficient as a component of a distributed IDS to warn of imminent attack.

High-interaction

  • Real system, with a full OS, services, and applications, instrumented and exposed to attackers.
  • More realistic target that can occupy an attacker for a long time.
  • But requires significantly more resources — and risk: it can be truly compromised.

Modern lightweight version

A canary token / honeytoken is a tiny honeypot: a fake document, API key, or decoy DB record that no one should ever touch. The moment someone uses it, an alert fires — high-signal detection with almost no upkeep.

09

From detecting to preventing

When observing isn't enough: block in real time.

Intrusion prevention systems

IPS / IDPS: the IDS that also acts

  • Also known as an IDPS (intrusion detection and prevention system).
  • It is an extension of an IDS that adds the ability to attempt to block or prevent detected malicious activity.
  • Can be host-based, network-based, or distributed/hybrid.
  • Uses anomaly detection (behavior unlike legitimate users) or signature/heuristic detection (known malicious behavior).
  • Can block traffic like a firewall, but uses IDS-style algorithms to decide when to do so.
The IPS dilemma

Because the IPS is inline and blocks automatically, a false positive now cuts off legitimate traffic — a self-inflicted denial of service. The base-rate fallacy stops being merely annoying and becomes dangerous. That's why many teams deploy in "detect-only" mode before turning on blocking.

Takeaways

Summary in one slide

  • Detection is the second line of defense in depth: prevent → detect → survive.
  • Two philosophies: anomaly (catches novel, noisy) and signature (catches known, precise). Deployed on the network or the host.
  • The base-rate fallacy is the central problem: when attacks are rare, false alarms dominate — even with a "99% accurate" IDS.
  • Honeypots and IPS/IDPS complete the picture; insertion, evasion, and DoS are how attackers beat the detector.
  • Today the IDS lives inside a SOC: Snort/Suricata/Zeek feed a SIEM (Splunk, Elastic, Sentinel); SOAR responds; EDR/XDR/NDR extend reach; MITRE ATT&CK maps detections to adversary techniques.
◆ Closing debate

Should detection ever replace prevention?

Your team can fund one improvement: a stricter inline firewall/IPS (which may cut legitimate traffic) or a more sensitive passive IDS (which produces 10,000 alarms/day). Which do you choose, and why?

To guide the debate

There's no right answer — it depends on the cost of each error. A hospital can tolerate false alarms but not a missed attack on lives; an online store can't afford to block legitimate customers. The point: this is an economic and risk decision, not a purely technical one — and the real-world answer is almost always "both, layered" with human triage and SIEM correlation.
